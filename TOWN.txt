THE TOWN
========

A plan for taking this from a strip mall in a wood to a town with a
strip mall on the edge of it: a street grid, a school, a church,
townhouses of two and three storeys, and an interior behind every door
that is drawn as a door.

WHAT IS BUILT OF IT, as of the commit this paragraph arrived in: all
seven phases, in the shape below and with the departures noted at the
end. The column is in js/level.js, the scale work in js/mapgeo.js and
js/fire.js, and the town itself in js/maps/town.js. Twenty-five blocks,
sixty runs of street with lamps down them and thirty-six junctions,
three hundred and twelve houses that are FACADES — a foundation, a
stoop, a door and windows cut into the wall, and no inside, which is the
largest of the departures below — a school with desks in its classrooms,
a church with pews and an altar under a choir loft, and a fire that
climbs through a ceiling. The rest of this document is the
plan it was built from and is left as it was written, so that what was
argued for and what was done can be compared.

WHERE IT DEPARTS FROM THE PLAN, and why:

  THE TOWN IS SOUTH, not north. The plan says north of the mall; the
    map's north is the back of the store and its wood, and the road
    into town runs east-west across the bottom of the car park. South
    is where a town on the other side of that road goes.
  A DETACHED HOUSE IS TWELVE TO A BLOCK, not thirty-two. The plan's own
    numbers distinguish a house lot (512 x 1152) from a townhouse
    (192 x 384) and it is the townhouse blocks that carry sixteen to a
    face. Twelve detached houses and thirty-two terraced ones is what
    the two lot sizes give.
  THE SCHOOL HAS SIX CLASSROOMS, not ten. Ten of 480 will not fit
    beside a gym of 1200 in a block of 3072, which the plan's own
    figures say if you add them up.
  THE SIDEWALK IS 112 AND THE KERB 12, and there is somewhere to park.
    The first cut had 144 | 24 | 240 | 24 | 144 at the user's request —
    a sidewalk three times the plan's and a kerb twice it, two travel
    lanes and nowhere to park; the second, at the user's request again,
    is 112 | 16 | 76 | 84 | 84 | 76 | 16 | 112 — a parking shoulder each
    side with the bays painted on it by the texture, a van in one bay in
    sixteen, storm drains in the gutters, and the kerb back at the
    plan's twelve. All of it out of the same 576, because the block pitch
    is what puts the mall on the grid. Main Street went to 1024 to keep
    a road in it at all. And THE KERB GOES ROUND THE CORNER: every
    junction's pavement corners are quarter circles, the streets stop at
    the wood in T's instead of running into it, and the two south
    corners of the town are bends in the road — see THE STREET, ROUNDED
    in README.txt.
  THE HOUSES HAVE NO INSIDES. THE TOWNHOUSE below, with its hall and
    stair and two bedrooms, was built, walked by the test from every
    front hall, and taken out again at the user's request — nobody went
    in, and a house at two in the morning is a thing you look at. A
    house is a shell now: one column of one storey, the roof, with a
    wall of solid pieces and RECESSES cut in it for the windows and the
    door, a foundation you can see, two steps up to the stoop and a path
    to the sidewalk. What was spent on wallpaper is spent on the street.
    See A HOUSE IS A FACADE in README.txt.
  THE ROOFS ARE STEEPER THAN THE PLAN'S and half the houses turn their
    gable to the street. Every roof was one in three, which cannot be
    seen from the pavement under it; a terrace is six in twelve now and
    a gable-fronted house nine in twelve, every eave wall has a soffit
    and a fascia board over the ground at its foot, and the gable band
    faces the street instead of the attic. See THE ROOF YOU CAN SEE in
    README.txt.
  THE SCHOOL HAS desks, lockers and windows you can see in through; the
    church has pews, a wainscot, an altar with a cross behind it and
    coloured glass you can see through. The two buildings with an inside
    got the inside.
  AND THEN THE STAIRS CAME OUT, at the user's request: the school's two
    and the church's switchback, all three of them. The first floor and
    the choir loft are still built, lit and furnished and there is no
    longer any way to walk to them; the fire reaches the floor above
    through a ceiling instead of up a chimney, which it still does, and
    to less of it. Written down rather than quietly lost.
  AND THEN THEY GOT AN OUTSIDE, at the user's request. Both of them
    were slabs of wall with holes in them, and what they have now is
    all one idea: a BAND, which is a strip of wall a projecting piece
    of geometry makes the disagreement rule draw. The school gets a
    stone water table, a string course, a cornice and twenty-four
    pilasters — the first three out of one rectangle whose column is
    open, shut, open, shut, shut — plus an entrance bay with real
    doors and a fitted-out gym. The church
    gets a water table, twelve buttresses in two stages, eaves down
    both long walls, a louvred belfry and a cornice under the spire,
    and inside it a nave OPEN TO THE ROOF with tie beams, a chancel
    arch, a communion rail, a pulpit and an organ on the loft. See
    A BUILDING IS WHAT IT DOES AT ITS EDGES in README.txt.
  THERE ARE FENCES, which the plan did not ask for and a town without
    them looks wrong: chain link round the ball field and along the
    school's frontage, wrought iron round the churchyard and the whole
    way round the cemetery with the gates where the paths are. A fence
    is a masked texture hung in the hole between two patches of open
    ground, not a sector — which is why every enclosure in the town has
    a VERGE outside it. A fence with nothing behind it has nothing to
    hang in, so the block is laid as a ring of verge, four gates and the
    ground inside, and the iron goes in the eight lines between them.
  THERE ARE LAMPS after all — one every 1536 along a sidewalk,
    staggered, one on every corner, each over a pool of light that is a
    brighter sector of pavement — and trees in the yards, shrubs along
    the foundations, paths across the parks and stones in the cemetery.
    None of it sectors; all of it sprites but the lamp, which is a
    PHOTOGRAPH stood flat across the street with its arm over the road
    (THE STREET LAMP IS GEOMETRY, js/mapgeo.js), and lit after dark by a
    cold tint on its pool and a flare at its head (js/lamplight.js). No
    utility poles still.
  AND IT IS DRAWN FOR A PHONE, which is a thing the town made hard and
    nobody checked until somebody played it on one. The portal flood
    that culls the shop so well was costing thirty-five milliseconds a
    frame out here, because an open street grid hides nothing and it was
    walking two hundred thousand lines to say so; it runs to four
    thousand units now and the frustum takes the rest. The crowd was a
    draw call each and is one per picture. A block past most of the air
    is not drawn, because at that range it is already the sky. See THE
    FRAME WAS MEASURED, AND THEN CUT in README.txt for the numbers.
  AND THE TOWN IS PLANTED, which is the difference between a street grid
    and a street. Six photographed broadleaves line every verge at a
    pitch of 512, off by a third between the two sides so the street
    reads as an avenue rather than a corridor, and never inside a lamp's
    pool. Clipped box edges the green, returns at every gate, runs along
    the school's foundation and inside the churchyard's iron. The
    cemetery has eight kinds of headstone dealt so the plain slabs are
    common and the obelisk and the crosses are not, laid in RANKS —
    jittered along the row and in the angle, never across it, because
    the line is what makes it a cemetery rather than a field with rubble
    in it — and the ranks step round three specimen trees a quarter.
    The art is photographs: see THE TWO ART PIPELINES in README.txt.
  A BLOCK OF BOX IS CANOPY, which sets the pitch of every hedge in the
    town at 64. The wood keeps one canopy plant to a cell and a cell is
    64 units, so a run planted tighter loses blocks to a rule it cannot
    see; a run planted looser has sky between one block and the next.
    The blocks are 67 wide and stand 64 apart, and where two runs meet
    the second one to ask for the corner is refused.
  THE FUEL IS THREE HUNDRED AND OVER in the school and the church,
    because the spread chance saturates at three hundred and a corridor
    at a hundred and twenty was a firebreak down the middle of the
    school. The houses, having no inside, do not burn; their yards do.
  THE STAIR IS A COLUMN OF STEPPED BOXES rather than a column of one
    with flights inside it. Tread i is floor 16i and ceiling 16i + 96,
    and the next flight stacks over it with the deck between — which is
    a staircase, and needs no special case anywhere.
  AND THE ROOF IS A SECTOR AFTER ALL. THE ROOF IS NOT A SECTOR below
    was written because a sector engine cannot slope a ceiling, and the
    first cut of the town took it at its word: the roofs were geometry
    and you could walk through one. A sector's floor or ceiling may now
    be a PLANE or a GABLE, so a roof is the top storey of a house's
    column and the engine knows where it is. WHAT THIS PLAN DOES NOT DO
    still stands on the ground being flat — there are no hills — but it
    no longer stands on the roofs being a picture.
  FIRE CLIMBS BY TWO PATHS. The plan gives it the stairwell; it has the
    stairwell, four times as willing as going along, and the CEILING as
    the slow way at a tenth of that. One flight reaches into the floor
    above at exactly one tread, and one cell cannot carry a house on
    its own. WITH THE STAIRS TAKEN OUT the second path is the only one
    the town uses, and it turns out to carry the school by itself: the
    corridor above and two classrooms off it catch, which is fewer than
    the chimney managed and is what a ceiling should look like.
  THE HALF-SECOND IS NOW ABOUT A SECOND AND A HALF. See THE TEST below
    for what was done about it and README.txt for the number.

Nothing below this line was built from. This is the plan, the numbers it
falls out of, the one engine idea it needs, and the order to do it in.

SIGHT.txt is its companion and covers what a town does to the renderer:
occlusion, fog, a sky that changes, levels of detail, the cull distance,
time of day and weather. Neither plan waits on the other; they meet at
exactly one place, which is the per-block batching in THE BLOCK IS THE
UNIT OF RESIDENCY below.

  the anchor                        3880 x 3400, x 200..4080, y 0..3400
  the fuel grid                     438 x 219 cells of 32  =  95,594
  the blockmap                      254 x 195 cells of 128 =  49,530
  distinct floor heights            0, 12, 40, 48, 80
  ceilings                          12 up to 640

Five floor heights and nothing above anything else. That last line is
the whole problem: the map is a floor plan, in the strict sense that
there is exactly one floor.

The pieces the town gets to inherit are not small. RectMap turns
rectangles into welded sectors and will do it for a hundred houses as
happily as for eleven shop units. `roadRun` and `roadBox` already draw a
straight and a junction with their lines painted on. The fire is a fuel
grid with a sparse active list, so a bigger grid costs memory and not
frames. `js/responders.js` has six tiers of people who turn up when the
alarm climbs, and tier 3 is called "the police" and tier 4 "the fire
brigade", and both of them currently arrive out of the wood from
nowhere.


WHAT THE TOWN IS FOR
--------------------

Three reasons, and if none of them held, the answer would be to make the
mall better instead.

THE FIRE HAS NOWHERE TO GO. The README's own list of what is not done
says it plainly: nothing follows you into the wood, and the wood's fire
and the store's do not cross the car park to each other. The car park is
a firebreak two hundred and eighty units wide and the ring road is
another three hundred, and that is the game's ceiling — you burn one
building and then you burn some trees. A town on the far side of the
ring road is the first thing this fire has ever been able to spread TO.
A terrace of timber-framed houses sharing party walls is the best fuel
in the world and every real fire service knows it.

THE RESPONDERS COME FROM NOWHERE. `ResponderState.arrive` has a comment
on it that says PLACEHOLDER, still: where a tier's wave would be put on
the road. A town has a fire station and a police station on it, at known
coordinates, with an engine standing in the bay. Tier 4 stops being a
timer and becomes a door opening four blocks away.

AND A SUPERMARKET IS A PLACE YOU GO FROM SOMEWHERE. The mall reads as
the anchor of a parade because there is a parade. It does not yet read
as the anchor of anything larger, because the thing a strip mall is
parasitic on — the town whose main street it killed — is not there. Put
the town in and the mall becomes what it is: the newest building for
miles and the only one with a car park.


THE SCALE, AND WHY THE GRID FALLS OUT OF IT
-------------------------------------------

The project already has a scale and it is written down: the cars are
drawn at THIRTY-TWO UNITS TO THE METRE, which is what makes the player's
56 units a metre seventy-five and a 64-pixel texture two metres of wall.
Everything below is that conversion and then rounded to a multiple of
16, and usually to a multiple of 64 so that a texture lands on a corner.

  one foot                    9.75 u      ->  use 16 where it must round
  a sidewalk, 5 ft            48.8        ->  48
  a verge, 5 ft               48.8        ->  48
  a parked car, 8 ft          78          ->  80
  a travel lane, 11.5 ft      112         ->  112
  a residential street, 60 ft 585         ->  576   (9 x 64)
  a main street, 80 ft        780         ->  768   (12 x 64)
  a block face, 315 ft        3072        ->  3072  (48 x 64)
  a house lot, 52 ft x 118 ft 512 x 1152
  a townhouse, 20 x 40 ft     192 x 384
  a storey, 9 ft clear        88          ->  96 clear + 16 of deck = 112
  an interior door, 6'8"      65          ->  64
  three storeys                                336
  a roof ridge over that                       +128  ->  464

That last number is worth stopping on. The mall's parapet is at 480. A
three-storey townhouse with a pitched roof tops out at 464. The town
skyline comes in SIXTEEN UNITS UNDER the supermarket, which is exactly
the relationship those two things have in life, and it was not arranged
— it is what 32 units to the metre gives you when you ask for three
storeys and a roof.

THE BLOCK PITCH IS 3648: a block of 3072 and a street of 576. And then:

  four pitches less one street   4 x 3648 - 576 = 13,968
  the mall's clearing across                      14,000
  two pitches less one street    2 x 3648 - 576 =  6,720
  the mall's clearing deep                         6,992

The strip mall, its car park and its ring road are ALREADY a four-by-two
superblock on a grid nobody has drawn yet, to within 32 units across and
272 deep. The 272 comes out of the yard fence line or out of the street
on its north side; either is one number in `js/maps/sellwrong.js`. This
is the single most useful fact in this document, because it means the
town does not have to be reconciled with the mall. The mall is already
on the grid.


THE GRID
--------

Five by five blocks of 3072 on a pitch of 3648, north of the mall.

  5 x 3648 + 576 = 18,816 across and the same deep — 588 metres, a bit
  over a third of a mile, which is a small town's whole built-up core

  the mall's superblock sits on the south edge: 13,968 x 6,720

  total built ground 18,816 x 26,112, against 14,000 x 6,992 today

MAIN STREET runs north out of the mall's ring road at 768 wide, through
the square, and stops at the school. It is the only street that is not
576 and it is the reason the town has a front.

The blocks, which is the part that makes it a town and not a lattice:

     E1 houses    E2 cemetery   E3 park,       E4 houses   E5 the grid
                                water tower                  runs out
     D1 houses    D2 houses     D3 houses      D4 houses   D5 houses
     C1 houses    C2 townhouse  C3 THE SCHOOL  C4 school   C5 houses
                     rows          and its yard   ball field
     B1 townhouse B2 library    B3 THE CHURCH  B4 townhouse B5 motel,
        rows         and flats     churchyard      rows        car lot
     A1 gas       A2 THE SQUARE A3 Main St     A4 Main St  A5 fire and
        station,     town hall     bank, post     hardware,   police
        diner        and green     office         laundromat  stations
     ------------------ the ring road ---------------------------------
     the mall, its car park, and eleven units of parade

Seventeen of the twenty-five blocks are residential or civic and eight
are the town's commercial spine and its services. The school takes two
blocks because an American school takes two blocks — a building and a
field, and the field is what tells you which it is from three streets
away.

MAIN STREET'S STOREFRONTS ARE TWO AND THREE STOREYS WITH FLATS OVER
THEM, which is the other half of the answer to "townhouses with two to
three levels". A block of Main Street is a party-wall terrace with shops
at the bottom and somebody living above, and it is the same construction
as the townhouse rows with a different ground floor. One building type,
two dressings.


THE STREET
----------

A residential street is 576 and it is nine bands:

  48 sidewalk | 48 verge | 80 parking | 112 lane | 112 lane |
  80 parking | 48 verge | 48 sidewalk                        = 576

Main Street is 768 and it is seven:

  112 sidewalk | 80 parking | 112 lane | 160 centre turn lane |
  112 lane | 80 parking | 112 sidewalk                        = 768

None of this needs new code. `roadRun` builds a straight as a run of
strips with the paint as textures and `roadBox` builds a junction with
no centre line and its edge lines turning the corner, and the ring road
round the car park is four of each. A five-by-five grid is 12 runs and
36 junctions, which is a double loop and a table.

WHAT IS NEW IS THE KERB AND THE SIDEWALK. The clearing has one kerb in
it (12 units, walked over without noticing) and no sidewalks at all. A
sidewalk is a sector 12 above the carriageway with a concrete flat and a
lower texture round it; the crossings are where it drops to 0 for 224
units. Both are one rectangle each in RectMap and the step-up rule makes
them free to walk.

AND THE LAMPS AND THE POLES. There are already 650 LAMP things in the
car park, so a street light is a thing type that exists. Utility poles
down one side of every residential street, with the wire between them as
two thin quads — the same trick `js/slidedoor.js` uses to draw a door
leaf that no sector knows about. Nothing in the world reads as America
faster than a wood pole with eleven wires on it, and it costs two
triangles.


THE ONE NEW IDEA: A SECTOR IS A COLUMN OF STOREYS
-------------------------------------------------

Doom's model has one floor at any (x,y), and `Level.sectorAt` returns one
sector, and `Actor.setSector` says `this.z = s.floor`, and that is the
end of it. A townhouse with a bedroom over a kitchen cannot be expressed.
There is no way round this that is not an engine change, so the question
is only which change.

WHAT WAS CONSIDERED AND PUT DOWN:

  Lay each storey out SIDE BY SIDE in map space and join them with a
  real staircase, the way half the multi-storey buildings in Doom's own
  levels are built. It works, it needs no engine at all, and it is
  wrong here for one reason: this game is about watching a building
  burn from outside it. A house whose first floor is two hundred units
  east of its ground floor cannot have its windows light up in order.

  Make every interior a SEPARATE LEVEL, entered at the door. This is
  the "level-to-level flow" the README lists as not done, and it has the
  same defect twice over: the fire cannot cross the threshold, and you
  cannot stand in the street and watch the house you just left go up.

  Import GZDoom's 3D FLOORS whole — arbitrary slabs projected into a
  region from a control sector elsewhere in the map. This is the real
  answer to the general problem and it is much more machinery than the
  specific problem needs. A townhouse's storeys share one footprint.
  The general case buys nothing and costs a control-sector concept that
  would sit in the map file being confusing for ever.

WHAT TO BUILD: a sector may name the sector ABOVE it, over the same
polygon. A column. `mb.column(poly, [ground, first, second])` makes
three sectors sharing one outline and links them, and every sector in
the game today is a column of one, so nothing that exists changes and no
existing map file gains a character.

  sectorAt(x, y)          unchanged — returns the GROUND sector
  spanAt(x, y, z)         new — walks the column and returns the storey
                            whose floor..ceil contains z, or the highest
                            one below it

A stairwell is a column of ONE, tall: floor 0, ceiling 336, with the
flights inside it as seven rects of 16 rise and 32 tread, which is a
Doom staircase and walks up without slowing down because MAX_STEP is 24.
Its neighbours are columns of three. That is the whole construction, and
it is why the column and not the slab: the shape of a house is a set of
columns, one of which is open all the way up.


A WALL IS WHERE TWO COLUMNS DISAGREE
------------------------------------

`MapBuilder._edge` welds two sectors onto one line and `assignLineTextures`
then decides the upper from whichever has the lower ceiling and the lower
from whichever has the higher floor. Two sectors, two surfaces, and Doom
called them upper and lower because two is all there ever were.

With columns there can be three storeys on one side and open air on the
other, and the honest statement of what to draw is:

  TAKE THE OPEN SPANS OF EACH COLUMN. THE WALL IS EVERY INTERVAL OF Z
  WHERE EXACTLY ONE OF THEM IS OPEN. WHERE BOTH ARE OPEN IS A HOLE AND
  WHERE NEITHER IS, THERE IS NOTHING TO DRAW.

Run that over a column of one against a column of one and out comes
Doom's upper and lower exactly, because the interval above the lower
ceiling IS the upper and the interval below the higher floor IS the
lower. Nothing about the mall changes. Run it over the street against a
three-storey terrace and out come three bands of brick with two ribbons
of glass between them, without the map file saying anything about bands.

It is about thirty lines in `assignLineTextures` and the same shape again
in `addLine`, and it replaces code rather than sitting beside it.

Collision follows: `Level.slideMove` already takes z and height and
already computes an opening from the two sectors — it becomes an opening
from the two SPANS at the mover's z, which is the same arithmetic with
`spanAt` in front of it.


WHAT THAT COSTS, FILE BY FILE
-----------------------------

  js/level.js         column(), spanAt(), the disagreement rule in
                        assignLineTextures, z-aware openings in
                        slideMove and the sight checks. The blockmap
                        does not change — it is indexed by x,y and a
                        column lives at one x,y.            ~200 lines
  js/mapgeo.js        addLine draws an interval list instead of an
                        upper and a lower; addFlats runs per storey;
                        a new roof() for the pitched caps.   ~150 lines
  js/maps/rectmap.js  a rect may carry `storeys: [props, props, ...]`
                        and calls column() instead of sector(). The
                        overlap check and the edge splitting are
                        unchanged, because they are about x and y.
                                                              ~40 lines
  js/actor.js         z is the span's floor, not the sector's; falling
                        and step-up consult spanAt.           ~60 lines
  js/fire.js          a grid per storey level, sparse, with two more
                        link bits for up and down.           ~120 lines
  js/ruin.js          a gutted storey drops its deck onto the one
                        below. Deferrable — see WHAT THIS PLAN DOES
                        NOT DO.
  tools/smoke-test.mjs  the new invariants. See THE TEST.

Roughly six hundred lines of engine against thirty-five thousand that
exist, and all of it in the two files that are already about exactly
this. That is the argument for the column over every alternative above:
it is a generalisation of the model rather than a second model beside it.

THE ROOF IS NOT A SECTOR. A sector engine cannot slope a ceiling and a
flat-roofed house is not an American house. So a pitched roof is
GEOMETRY, generated from the building's footprint — two sloped quads and
two gable triangles — and pushed into the same BatchSet as everything
else. There is precedent for this in the project and it is load-bearing:
`roofFraming` in `js/ruin.js` already puts a steel frame over a burnt-out
region without any sector knowing, and the logo over the entrance is
geometry for the same reason. You never get on a roof; it does not need
to be walkable; it needs to be a silhouette.


THE TOWNHOUSE
-------------

192 across, 384 deep, party walls both sides, a stoop on the front and a
yard at the back. Two storeys or three, which differ by one more storey
in the column and one more flight in the stairwell — the same floor plan
file with a length argument.

  GROUND   stoop 192x64 outside the front door
           hall and stair 64 x 384, running back from the door
           front room 128 x 160
           kitchen and diner 128 x 176
           WC under the stair 64 x 48
           back door to the yard
  FIRST    landing 64 x 96
           front bedroom 128 x 160
           back bedroom 128 x 128
           bathroom 128 x 48
  SECOND   the same again, with the back bedroom a box room

About thirty-two sectors a house counting the stair flights, which is
a tenth of the entire map as it stands today, for one house. Sixteen
houses to a block face and two faces back to back is thirty-two houses
and a thousand sectors A BLOCK. This number is the reason for the
residency section below and it should be read before anyone starts
building blocks.

FOUR PLANS AND THREE DRESSINGS, not thirty-two hand-drawn houses. The
plans differ in where the stair is and whether the kitchen is through or
galley; the dressings are brick, clapboard and vinyl siding with
different trim. Mirror half of them. It is a terrace: they are SUPPOSED
to be the same house eight times, and the eye reads the roofline and the
paint, which is where the variation should go.

WHAT IS IN THEM. The shoppers already stand about in regions and there
are 736 of them; a house wants furniture, and furniture in this engine
is a raised floor with a lower texture round it, which is what every
fixture in the supermarket is. A bed is a sector 40 up wearing a bed
picture. A sofa is one 32 up. A table is 48. The produce bins in the
store are already exactly this and their tops are their own 64x64
pictures, so the machinery and the taste are both settled.


THE SCHOOL
----------

Two storeys over a footprint of about 2400 x 1600, on block C3, with the
ball field on C4 — and the field is what identifies it, so the field gets
built first and gets its backstop and its baselines as floor texture the
way the parking bays are floor texture.

  a double-loaded CORRIDOR the length of the building, 128 wide, with
    lockers as a lower texture 64 tall down both sides — the corridor is
    the school, the way an aisle is the supermarket
  ten CLASSROOMS off it, 480 x 384, desks as raised floors in rows
  the GYM, 1200 x 800, two storeys tall in one span, which the column
    model gets for nothing: it is a column of one that is 224 tall where
    its neighbours are two of 112
  the CAFETERIA and its kitchen
  the OFFICE at the front door, and a nurse's room
  upstairs: more classrooms, a library, a lab

The gym is the argument for columns over storeys-as-a-fixed-stack in one
picture: a building is not N floors, it is a set of columns some of which
are taller than others.


THE CHURCH
----------

Coursed stone, a gable end to the street, a tower with a spire. Block
B3, with a churchyard and a parish hall behind it.

  NARTHEX at the door, 320 x 160
  NAVE 640 x 960, ceiling at 336 — three storeys of air in one column,
    with pews as raised floors 32 up in two banks
  CHANCEL raised 24, three steps up
  the CHOIR LOFT over the narthex at 224 — a real second storey over a
    real ground floor, reached by a stair in the tower, and the first
    place in the game where you can look down on a room you were just
    standing in
  the TOWER: three levels of 112 and then the spire, which is geometry
    like the roofs are. The tower breaks 480 and it is the only thing in
    the town that does, so the outdoor sky ceiling goes to 768 for the
    town's sectors — one number, and the water tower on E3 needs it too

A church is the best-lit building in a town at night and the last thing
anyone expects to burn, and it is made of painted wood.

THE BLOCK IS THE UNIT OF RESIDENCY
----------------------------------

A thousand sectors a residential block against 327 in the whole map
today. Twenty-five blocks fully built is somewhere between eight and
fifteen thousand sectors and thirty to fifty thousand lines. Three things
break at that size and all three break quietly.

STARTUP. The game's promise is that it builds everything in the page in
about half a second. Building the town's geometry is linear and fine;
the fuel grid is not, because `Fire` calls `sectorAt` once per cell to
find out what each cell is standing in, and at 32-unit cells over
18,816 x 26,112 that is 480,000 point-in-polygon queries against the
blockmap. Fix: rasterise the sectors INTO the grid instead of querying
per cell — walk each sector's bbox once and fill. Same answer, one pass,
and it is the difference between three hundred milliseconds and thirty.

CULLING. `buildLevelGeometry` batches by texture, one mesh per texture
for the whole level, and sets `frustumCulled = true` on each. A batch
whose bounding sphere is the entire town is never culled, so every
brick wall in the town is submitted every frame. Fix: batch per BLOCK
and per texture. Twenty-five blocks by twenty textures is more draw
calls than twenty, and it is the only way the frustum means anything.

THE CHAR REBUILD. When a region finishes burning, the static geometry is
rebuilt IN FULL — "a few thousand triangles and about ten milliseconds,
perhaps twenty times in a level". At town scale that is a hundred
thousand triangles and a visible hitch, twenty times, during the exact
moments the game is at its best. Per-block batching fixes this too and
for the same reason: rebuild the block that charred.

AND THE INTERIORS ARE LOD. The buffer is two to four hundred rows tall.
You cannot see through a 48-unit window at forty metres at that
resolution — there is nothing there to see. So a house beyond a
threshold contributes its SHELL only: outer walls, roof, window
rectangles as a texture rather than as a hole. Its interior sectors are
not in any batch. They come in when you are close enough to look through
the door, which is the same trick `js/forest.js` already plays on fifty
thousand trees and which the test already counts.

None of this is optional and none of it is hard. It is all in
`js/mapgeo.js` and it wants doing BEFORE there are twenty-five blocks,
not after.

AND THE FRUSTUM IS NOT THE CULL THAT MATTERS. Batching per block is what
makes a cull POSSIBLE; the frustum is only the weakest one that can then
be applied, and on a street grid it keeps every block in front of you
whether or not a house is standing in the way. The cull this town
actually wants is a portal flood over the sector graph, which is exact,
which the data model already has the input for, and which is SIGHT.txt's
business rather than this document's. What matters here is that the two
plans agree on the unit: the flood produces visible SECTORS, this plan
batches by BLOCK, and the drawing is the blocks those sectors are in.


FIRE IN A BUILDING WITH STAIRS
------------------------------

The fuel grid is 32-unit cells with heat, fuel and a four-bit link for
north, south, east and west. Give it two more bits, up and down, and one
grid per storey level, and fire climbs.

This is the best thing the town does for the game and it is nearly free.
A fire on a ground floor finds the stairwell — a column open all the way
up — and goes up it, because that is what the link bits now allow and
because it is what fire does. The first floor lights from the stairwell
and not from the front room, which is the correct and horrible answer.

The grids are SPARSE: allocated per block, and only for the storey
levels that block actually has. Most of the town's area is street and
yard and has one level, so the memory is not three times 480,000 cells,
it is 480,000 plus the footprint of the buildings twice, which is about
a fifth more.

PARTY WALLS. A terrace burns end to end through the party wall, slowly,
and that is a link bit across the 16 units of wall void with a resistance
on it. This is the difference between burning a house and burning a
street, and it is one flag on one kind of wall.

FUEL FIGURES. The store's table has a car park at zero and a warehouse at
the top. A house is more flammable per square metre than a supermarket —
timber frame, carpets, curtains, furniture, and a fraction of the
sprinklered floor area. Domestic interiors go in high; brick shells go in
at nearly nothing, so a terrace burns out and stands there as a row of
walls with a steel-less roof gone, which is what `js/ruin.js` is for.


WHAT IT DOES TO THE SYSTEMS THAT ALREADY WORK
---------------------------------------------

  THE WOOD. Forest reach is 9000 units on every side of a clearing
    14,000 across. Round a town 18,816 x 26,112 that is 50,000 trees
    becoming something like 135,000 at the same density. Do not pay it:
    the town blocks its own sightlines, so cut the reach to about 4,000
    on the three town sides and keep 9,000 behind the mall where the
    road runs out and where the view is the whole point.
  THE ROAD OUT. `roadEnds` is two points where the through road leaves
    the map heading west and east. The grid connects to it at the ring
    road and the ends stay exactly where they are.
  THE RESPONDERS. `arrive()` picks a point from a list and the comment
    on it says PLACEHOLDER. It gets the fire station on A5 and the
    police station beside it. Six tiers become six doors.
  THE CROWD. 736 shoppers stand in the store's regions. The town is not
    populated at night and should not be — a street with nobody on it is
    correct at two in the morning and is also free. What the town wants
    instead is LIGHT IN WINDOWS, and a window that goes dark when its
    room burns.
  THE VEHICLES. 36 vans in the lot and a police van that drives a
    polyline. The town's streets are polylines. Parked cars down both
    sides of every residential street are the same placement arithmetic
    as the bays, against a kerb instead of a painted line.
  THE GUNSHIP. It climbs over the parapet rather than avoiding it and
    ignores everything but the ground. Over a town of pitched roofs and
    a church spire this stops being a quirk and starts being a thing
    flying through a steeple. It wants the buildings in its height map.
  THE SKY CEILING. Outdoor sectors cap at 480 and the church tower and
    the water tower go past it. One number, town-wide: 768.


THE ART
-------

The 64-pixel rule stands and the smoke test enforces it, so every one of
these is a 64x64 or 64x128 generated in `js/textures.js` beside the
hundred and forty-seven that exist. What tiles is MATERIAL and never a
word — the README's hardest-won lesson, paid for by SELLWRONG SUPERSTORE
appearing across the fascia sixty times.

  outside    red brick, painted brick, clapboard, vinyl siding, stucco,
               asphalt shingle, standing-seam metal, a shingled gable
  street     concrete sidewalk with a scored joint, granite kerb,
               asphalt in two ages, crosswalk bars, a manhole
  civic      school brick with a limestone band, church coursed stone,
               stained glass, a chain-link fence, a backstop
  inside     plaster, wallpaper in three papers, tile, carpet, lino,
               floorboards, a stair tread, a skirting, a lockers run,
               a pew end, a blackboard
  fixtures   bed, sofa, table, counter, desk, bath, kitchen worktop

About forty new textures, which is a third again of the bank, and they
are the cheapest part of this plan by a distance: 177ms bakes the
existing hundred and forty-seven.

WINDOWS ARE THE ONE THAT MATTERS. A town at night is a grid of lit
rectangles and nothing else reads. A window is already expressible — a
line with a hole between an upper and a lower — and with columns it
works per storey without a new idea. What it needs is for the light in
the room behind it to reach the glass, and the sky/light split already
in `js/mapgeo.js` is the place that happens.


HOW IT IS BUILT — THE ORDER
---------------------------

Each phase ends with something you can walk around in. Nothing here
requires the phase after it to have been thought about.

  1  THE COLUMN, against the map that exists.
     column(), spanAt(), the disagreement rule, z-aware collision,
     per-storey flats and walls. Prove it by putting a MEZZANINE over
     the supermarket's back-of-house — one room over one room, with a
     stair — and shipping that alone. If the column is wrong, it is
     wrong here, where the map is 327 sectors and the test is green.

  2  MAPGEO AT SCALE, before there is any scale.
     Per-block batching, per-block rebuild, the shell/interior LOD,
     and the fuel grid by rasterisation instead of by query. Measured
     against the existing map, where the numbers should not move.

  3  THE GRID AND ONE BLOCK.
     The street runs, the junctions, the sidewalks, the kerbs, the
     poles and the lamps for all twenty-five blocks — the streets are
     cheap and they are what makes the place legible. Then ONE
     residential block built completely: thirty-two townhouses, four
     plans, three dressings, every interior. This is the vertical
     slice and it is where the real cost of a block is learned.

  4  FIRE THAT CLIMBS.
     Per-storey grids, the up and down link bits, party walls, the
     domestic fuel table. Burn the block from phase 3 down. This is
     the phase that tells you whether the whole idea was worth it,
     and it should happen before there are twenty-five blocks and not
     after.

  5  THE SCHOOL AND THE CHURCH.
     The two buildings with a shape of their own, and the two that
     want hand work rather than a plan generator. The gym and the
     nave both exercise the tall-column case; the choir loft and the
     tower exercise the stacked case.

  6  THE REST OF THE BLOCKS.
     Residential multiplied out, Main Street's terraces with shops
     under flats, and then the named buildings — fire station and
     police station first, because they turn `arrive()` from a
     placeholder into a door.

  7  WHAT THE TOWN MAKES POSSIBLE.
     Responders from real stations. Light in windows. The gas station
     forecourt. A fire that crosses the ring road in both directions.

Phases 1 and 2 are engine and are the risk. Phase 3 is the one that
takes the longest. Phases 5 to 7 are content and can be done in any
order by anybody.


THE TEST
--------

1749 checks pass today in about three minutes, with no install and no
browser, and every one of them is there because something reached a
screenshot. The column breaks assumptions that nothing currently
asserts, so these come first, in phase 1, before the code they check:

  every column's storeys stack without overlap or gap, and each one's
    ceiling is its neighbour's floor less the deck
  spanAt agrees with sectorAt for every column of one — the whole
    existing map, which is the regression that matters
  the disagreement rule reproduces upper/lower exactly on every line
    of the existing map, texture for texture
  no line is left with a surface of zero height or of negative height
  every storey above the ground is reachable: a walk from the START
    thing reaches every column's every storey, which is the check that
    catches a stairwell whose flights were laid out one rect short
  no interior sector is outside the shell that is supposed to contain
    it, which is the townhouse generator's own correctness
  the fuel grid rasterisation returns the same sectorOf for every cell
    as the per-cell query it replaces — run both, compare, once
  a fire lit on a ground floor reaches the top storey, and one lit in
    a house with the stair door shut does not
  the town builds in a budget, asserted in milliseconds, because the
    half-second promise is the one thing here that cannot be argued
    back later

And the block counts — sectors, lines, triangles, draw calls, grid cells
— printed as notes rather than asserted, the way the gunship's node count
and the music's bpm are printed, so that the cost of a block is visible
in every test run and nobody has to go looking for the day it doubled.


WHAT THIS PLAN DOES NOT DO
--------------------------

  It does not slope THE GROUND. A floor or a ceiling may be a plane or
    a gable now — see the note at the top of this file — and the roofs
    are built out of that, but no street rises and no block sits above
    another. A flat American town is common enough that this is a
    choice and not a defect, but it is a choice.
  It does not collapse a storey onto the one below. `js/ruin.js` drops
    a roof and leaves its steel; a gutted first floor landing in the
    kitchen is the same idea one level down and is deliberately left
    out of phases 1 to 7. It is the obvious phase 8 and it is the best
    thing this engine could do that no other Doom-alike does.
  It does not put anybody in the town. No residents, no cars moving, no
    crowd. Two in the morning is the excuse and it is a good one for a
    first pass; it will not survive a second.
  It does not give the player a reason to go into most of the houses.
    Interiors behind every door is what was asked for and it is what
    makes a street real from the outside; what is IN them, beyond
    furniture and fuel, is a game design question this plan does not
    answer.
  It does not address save, and the town makes the absence worse — an
    18,816-unit town is a longer night than a supermarket.
  It leaves the wood's fire and the buildings' fire still meeting only
    where the player carries it, unless phase 7 is done properly.
